## Clinical summary

**Bottom line**
The retrieved evidence supports **exercise interventions** (reported to improve motor skills) and **naturalistic developmental behavioral interventions (NDBIs)** (feasible for preschoolers) as effective approaches for children with autism spectrum disorder (ASD). A **modified ketogenic diet combined with rehabilitation** showed short-term reductions in behavioral and symptom severity, but further validation is needed.

**Most important recent finding**
Exercise significantly improved overall, gross, and fine motor skills in children with ASD, with dose-response relationships identified for overall and gross motor skills. [1]

**Immediate context**
While behavioral and dietary interventions show promise for specific outcomes (e.g., behavioral symptoms, motor skills), no single approach is universally established for all aspects of ASD. Evidence for feeding difficulties (indirectly related) and mindfulness-based parenting support is limited or under investigation.

## Latest findings
- **2026 Dec**: Systematic review found virtual reality (VR) and augmented reality (AR) interventions significantly increased research activity in autism, with VR as the largest modality (n = 61) and hybrid systems (n = 49) as the most common functional category [3].
- **2026-09-18**: Meta-analysis showed exercise significantly improved overall, gross, and fine motor skills in children with autism spectrum disorder (ASD), with dose-response relationships identified for overall and gross motor skills [1].
- **2026-09-11**: Randomized controlled trial demonstrated that a modified ketogenic diet combined with rehabilitation training led to greater short-term reductions in ABC and CARS scores compared to rehabilitation alone in young children with ASD [4].
- **2026-07-23**: Pilot randomized controlled trial suggested caregiver-assisted group-based Naturalistic Developmental Behavioral Intervention (NDBI) is a promising early intervention option for autistic preschoolers, though improvements in nonverbal development were limited to one-on-one interventions [6].

## Key studies

1. **Wang et al. (2026)**, meta-analysis: “Exercise significantly improved overall, gross, and fine motor skills in children with ASD.” [1]
2. **Hao et al. (2026)**, systematic review: “There was a significant increase in XR autism research after 2020, with virtual reality (VR) as the largest modality (n = 61, 53.0%), followed by augmented reality (AR) (n = 36, 31.3%).” [3]
3. **Sandbank et al. (2023)**, meta-analysis: “When effects were then restricted to exclude those at high risk of detection bias, only one significant summary effect was estimated-naturalistic developmental behavioral interventions on measures of diagnostic characteristics of autism (0.30, 0.03 to 0.57; P=0.03).” [2]
4. **Zeidan et al. (2022)**, systematic review: “Prevalence estimates increased over time and varied greatly within and across sociodemographic groups.” [7]
5. **Siafis et al. (2022)**, meta-analysis: “There were some indications of improvement by carnosine, haloperidol, folinic acid, guanfacine, omega-3-fatty-acids, probiotics, sulforaphane, tideglusib and valproate, yet imprecise and not robust.” [5]

## Conflicting evidence
None was retrieved.

## What remains under investigation
- **Efficacy**: Head-to-head comparisons of behavioral interventions (e.g., NDBI vs. traditional therapy) and long-term outcomes of extended reality (XR) interventions in ASD.
- **Effectiveness**: Real-world effectiveness of modified ketogenic diets and pharmacological treatments (e.g., bumetanide) in diverse clinical settings.
- **Feasibility**: Scalability of caregiver-assisted group-based interventions for broader implementation in resource-limited settings.
- **Association**: Neurophysiological markers (e.g., sleep spindle characteristics) as predictors of treatment response in ASD.
- **Exploratory**: Dose-response relationships for pharmacological agents (e.g., atomoxetine) and sensory interventions (e.g., Vitamin-B6 for sensory reactivity).

## Evidence limitations

- Findings are drawn from abstracts, registry records and web pages, not full texts; study quality was not appraised from the full papers.
- Doses appear only where an FDA label gives them for this condition (section 2.1); for any other use, see a prescribing source. Approval dates appear only where a retrieved source states them.
- Excluded retracted publication: Efficacy of oral folinic acid supplementation in children with autism spectrum disorder: a (PMID 39243316, 2024). Retracted work is not used as evidence.
