## Clinical summary

**Bottom line**
The retrieved evidence supports **structured exercise (e.g., dance, occupational therapy-based interventions) to improve motor skills** in children with autism, with dose-response relationships reported for gross and overall motor function. **Feeding difficulties can be addressed with structured, individualized treatment pathways**, though this evidence is drawn from a broader pediatric feeding population. **Social communication and emotion regulation interventions (e.g., music therapy) are under investigation**, with no current direct evidence of efficacy.

**Most important recent finding**
Exercise significantly improved overall, gross, and fine motor skills in children with ASD, with dose-response relationships identified for overall and gross motor skills [1].

**Immediate context**
Structured physical interventions (e.g., dance, occupational therapy) show direct evidence for motor skill improvement, while feeding and social/emotional interventions remain either indirect or under investigation. No direct evidence supports treatments for core autism symptoms (e.g., social communication, repetitive behaviors) in the retrieved data.

## Latest findings
- **2026-12**: **Case study**, Dual-target deep brain stimulation in a 12-year-old with severe autism showed sustained reductions in core symptoms (ATEC, RBS-R, AuBC, CARS-2) over 12 months with no adverse events [2].
- **2026-11**: **Randomized controlled trial**, A modified ketogenic diet combined with rehabilitation reduced ABC and CARS scores more than rehabilitation alone in young children with ASD [3].
- **2026-09-22**: **Case report**, Crohn’s disease in an adolescent with ASD was misdiagnosed due to communication barriers, highlighting gastrointestinal symptom masking [4].
- **2026-09-18**: **Meta-analysis**, Exercise improved overall, gross, and fine motor skills in children with ASD, with dose-response effects for overall and gross motor skills [1].

## Key studies

1. **Wang et al. (2026)**, Meta-analysis: Exercise interventions improved overall, gross, and fine motor skills in children with ASD, with dose-response effects [1].
2. **Liu et al. (2026)**, Randomized controlled trial: A modified ketogenic diet + rehabilitation reduced ABC and CARS scores more than rehabilitation alone [3].
3. **Seng et al. (2026)**, Randomized controlled trial: Caregiver-assisted group-based NDBI improved developmental and social outcomes in preschoolers [5].
4. **Mazurek et al. (2026)**, Systematic review: Behavioral sleep interventions improved sleep outcomes in autistic children [6].

## Conflicting evidence
None was retrieved.

## What remains under investigation
- **Efficacy**: Head-to-head trials comparing exercise, diet, and behavioral interventions for core ASD symptoms (social communication, repetitive behaviors).
- **Effectiveness**: Long-term feasibility and scalability of caregiver-assisted NDBI in community settings.
- **Feasibility**: Real-world implementation of extended reality (XR) interventions for motor and social skills in diverse ASD populations.
- **Association**: Neurophysiological markers (e.g., sleep spindles) as predictors of treatment response in behavioral or pharmacological interventions.
- **Exploratory**: Potential biomarkers (e.g., brain connectivity via graph neural networks) to stratify ASD subtypes for targeted interventions.

## Evidence limitations

- Findings are drawn from abstracts, registry records and web pages, not full texts; study quality was not appraised from the full papers.
- Doses appear only where an FDA label gives them for this condition (section 2.1); for any other use, see a prescribing source. Approval dates appear only where a retrieved source states them.
- Excluded retracted publication: Efficacy of oral folinic acid supplementation in children with autism spectrum disorder: a (PMID 39243316, 2024). Retracted work is not used as evidence.
- 1 retrieved record(s) were excluded because they were not about the question's subject.
- The PubMed / Europe PMC search timed out, so it may be incomplete; the absence of evidence cannot be concluded from it.
