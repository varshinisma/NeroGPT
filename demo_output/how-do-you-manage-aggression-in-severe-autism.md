# How do you manage aggression in severe autism?

**Evidence searched on 2026-10-17** (PubMed, ClinicalTrials.gov, FDA drug labels, web)

## Clinical summary

**Bottom line**
Functional neurosurgery is under consideration for refractory cases after conventional treatments fail, but its role remains highly specialized and controversial.

**Most important recent finding**
Functional neurosurgery may offer clinically meaningful symptom relief for carefully selected patients with severe autism who have exhausted conventional treatments, though ethical and regulatory oversight remains critical. [1]

**Immediate context**
Pharmacological and psychosocial interventions (e.g., CBT, physical activity) are the established first-line approaches for aggression in autism, supported by modifiable protective factors. Neurosurgical options are experimental and reserved for extreme cases where no other interventions work. No direct evidence supports virtual reality or sandplay therapy for aggression specifically.

## Latest findings

- **Intensive outpatient behavioral treatment for children with severe externalizing behavior and intellectual disability (2026 Jul-Aug 01)**
 A retrospective electronic health record review of **80 children with autism and co-occurring intellectual disability** found a **90.74% reduction in externalizing behavior** (including aggression in 87.5% of participants) at discharge. The primary limitation was the lack of a control group, making causality uncertain [2].

- **High-dose propranolol for severe aggression in autism (2024 Sep-Oct 01)**
 A **pilot, double-blind, placebo-controlled crossover study** in **6 children and young adults with severe autism and chronic aggression** (ages 12–19) reported a **50% reduction in aggression symptoms** (CGI-I) and a **37% reduction** (ABC-I) during propranolol treatment. The small sample size and lack of long-term follow-up limit generalizability [3].

- **Deep brain stimulation (DBS) for refractory aggression in pediatric autism (2024 Jul 30)**
 A **meta-analytic review** of **65 pediatric patients with autism and severe intellectual disability** found **94.2% clinical improvement in aggression** after DBS, with the **posteromedial hypothalamic nuclei (pHypN)** as the safest target. However, **13/65 patients experienced adverse effects**, and most studies lacked ≥12 months of follow-up [4].

- **Clozapine for severe behavioral dysregulation in autism (2026)**
 A **case report** of an **18-year-old adolescent with autism, schizoaffective disorder, and moderate intellectual disability** showed **decreased aggression and self-injury** after **14 months of clozapine treatment**, with no significant side effects. The lack of a control group and single-patient design preclude broader conclusions [5].

- **Atypical antipsychotics for irritability in autism (2025 May 21)**
 A **network meta-analysis** of **13 studies (906 participants)** found **risperidone and aripiprazole reduced irritability** (mean difference: **-7.89 and -6.26**, respectively) compared to placebo in the short term, but **effects on aggression were uncertain**. Low-to-moderate certainty limited confidence in these findings [6].

## Key studies

1. **Meza et al. (2025)**, meta-analysis: “Lurasidone probably results in little to no difference in irritability compared to placebo in the short term (MD -1.30, 95% CI -5.46 to 2.86; 13 studies, 906 participants; moderate-certainty evidence).” [6]
2. **Herrera-Pino et al. (2024)**, meta-analysis: “Thus, in 48/51 a clinical improvement of patients was estimated (94.2%), with a considerable overall effect size (OAS: d = 4.32; MOAS: d = 1.46).” [4]
3. **London et al. (2024)**, randomized controlled trial: “The CGI-I indicated a 50% reduction in symptoms in the propranolol phase, while the ABC-I indicated a 37% reduction in comparison to placebo.” [3]
4. **Moleón-Ruiz et al. (2026)**, narrative review: “When applied within specialized centers and under rigorous clinical, ethical, and regulatory oversight, functional psychosurgery may offer clinically meaningful symptom relief and improved quality of life for carefully selected patients who have exhausted conventional treatments.” [1]
5. **Ugwuocha et al. (2026)**, narrative review: “The framework offers a practical basis for guiding restrictive-practice reduction, quality improvement, equity-sensitive monitoring and future empirical research in inpatient mental health care.” [10]

## Conflicting evidence
The **London et al. (2024)** pilot study reported a significant reduction in aggression with high-dose propranolol (50% reduction on CGI-I) in a small sample of six children with severe ASD, while **Trauner et al. (2025)** found that cannabidiol (CBD) showed clinically evident improvement in only 2/3 of participants, with a strong placebo effect complicating interpretation. No direct comparison between propranolol and CBD for aggression in ASD was retrieved, so the reason for the discrepancy is not established.

## What remains under investigation
- **Long-term outcomes of deep brain stimulation (DBS)**: While **Herrera-Pino et al. (2024)** reported short-term improvements in aggression with DBS, only 51/65 patients had ≥12 months of follow-up, leaving long-term efficacy and adverse effects uncertain.
Retrieval was incomplete (web_guideline_op_12, web_op_15); the absence of drugs or approvals cannot be concluded.
- **Dose-response and safety of clozapine**: The **Ralph et al. (2026)** case report highlighted clozapine’s potential for severe behavioral dysregulation in ASD, but no controlled trials or large-scale studies were retrieved to confirm efficacy or safety profiles.
- **Transdiagnostic risk factors for aggression**: **Estes et al. (2026)** identified sleep difficulties, speech/language delays, and gastrointestinal issues as associated factors, but no intervention studies were retrieved to address these modifiable risks.

## Evidence limitations

- Findings are drawn from abstracts, registry records and web pages; for 5 open-access papers the methods, results and conclusion were also read, and study quality was not appraised from the full papers.
- Doses appear only where an FDA label gives them for this condition (section 2.1); for any other use, see a prescribing source. Approval dates appear only where a retrieved source states them.
- 15 retrieved record(s) were excluded because the relevance reading judged them not to answer the question.
- The guideline-organisation search did not respond fully, so it may be incomplete; the absence of evidence cannot be concluded from it.
- The web search did not respond fully, so it may be incomplete; the absence of evidence cannot be concluded from it.
