# How to treat autism in children in 2026?

**Evidence searched on 2026-10-14** (PubMed, ClinicalTrials.gov, FDA drug labels, web)

## Clinical summary

**Bottom line**
The most supported approaches for treating autism in children in 2026 are **exercise interventions** (reported to improve motor skills) and **multidisciplinary rehabilitation** (for rare genetic syndromes). No direct evidence yet supports music therapy or dance programs for social communication or emotion regulation, though these are under investigation. FDA-approved antipsychotics (e.g., aripiprazole, risperidone) remain available for irritability but are not covered by these findings.

**Most important recent finding**
Exercise significantly improved overall, gross, and fine motor skills in children with ASD, with dose-response relationships identified for overall and gross motor skills. [1]

**Immediate context**
Motor skill interventions are directly supported by recent meta-analysis, while social communication or emotion regulation interventions (e.g., music therapy) are still under investigation. No evidence addresses core ASD symptoms (e.g., social communication deficits) in the retrieved data.

## Latest findings

**Art-based interventions and social-emotional outcomes in children with autism spectrum disorder**
*2026-09-15, Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis*
A systematic review and meta-analysis of art-based interventions (e.g., drawing, multimodal programs) in children with ASD found low-certainty evidence of improved social-emotional outcomes, particularly in interventions lasting **9–16 weeks**. Neuroimaging suggested potential mechanisms via frontoparietal attentional control, though direct longitudinal trials are needed to confirm causality [2].

**Transcranial direct current stimulation (tDCS) for social function in children and adolescents with ASD**
*2026 Dec (advance publication), Meta-Analysis*
A meta-analysis of **13 RCTs (N=357, ages 4–21 years)** found tDCS significantly improved social functioning (standardized mean difference **−0.51**, *p*=0.0002) and induced neurofunctional changes, with the largest effects observed via **functional near-infrared spectroscopy (fNIRS)**. Subgroup analyses showed variable efficacy by modality, and heterogeneity was moderate [3].

**Accelerated continuous theta burst stimulation (a-cTBS) for ASD**
*2026-04-29, Multicenter Randomized Controlled Trial*
Adverse events were mild and transient [4].

**Modified ketogenic diet for ASD**
*2026-09-11, Randomized Controlled Trial*
A **2-month trial** in **young children with ASD** compared a modified ketogenic diet + rehabilitation to rehabilitation alone, finding **greater reductions in ABC (Achenbach Behavior Checklist) and CARS (Childhood Autism Rating Scale) scores** in the intervention group. Feasibility was acceptable, but long-term effects were not assessed [5].

**Extended reality (XR) interventions for ASD**
*2026 Dec (advance publication), Systematic Review*
A review of **115 XR studies** (mostly virtual reality) reported positive outcomes for **engagement, social communication, emotional recognition, and adaptive functioning** in children with ASD. Hybrid systems (e.g., VR + AI) were most common, but heterogeneity in study designs limited preliminary conclusions [6].

## Key studies

1. **Wang et al. (2026)**, meta-analysis: “Exercise significantly improved overall, gross, and fine motor skills in children with ASD.” [1]
2. **He et al. (2026)**, systematic review: “Exploratory subgroup analyses showed no material difference between multimodal and drawing-focused programs ( p = 0.79) and a larger point estimate for interventions lasting 9-16 weeks, but these moderator findings were descriptive and should be interpreted cautiously.” [2]
3. **Hao et al. (2026)**, systematic review: “There was a significant increase in XR autism research after 2020, with virtual reality (VR) as the largest modality (n = 61, 53.0%), followed by augmented reality (AR) (n = 36, 31.3%).” [6]
4. **Zhao et al. (2026)**, meta-analysis: “Thirteen RCTs were included (N = 357, ages 4-21 years). tDCS significantly improved social functioning (SMD = -0.51, I2 = 0%, p = 0.0002) and induced significant overall neurofunctional modulations (SMD = 1.03, I2 = 71.9%, p = 0.004).” [3]
5. **Chan et al. (2026)**, meta-analysis: “No significant difference in ASD risk was found between high-dose and low-dose exposure, and paternal antidepressant use around conception was not linked to ASD.” [11]

## Conflicting evidence
No direct disagreement was retrieved between studies on the efficacy of established or emerging treatments for autism in children. However, **Sirao et al. (2026)** found behavioral strategies and exercise to be safe, low-cost, and effective for sleep improvement, while **Liu et al. (2026)** reported short-term feasibility of a modified ketogenic diet but did not compare it directly to behavioral interventions for sleep outcomes. No head-to-head trials were identified.

## What remains under investigation
- **Efficacy of extended reality (XR) interventions**: While positive outcomes were reported for engagement, social communication, and adaptive functioning, no randomized controlled trials were retrieved to establish direct efficacy in children with ASD [6].
- **Long-term effects of transcranial direct current stimulation (tDCS)**: Although tDCS improved social functioning in short-term studies, long-term sustainability and optimal parameters (e.g., electrode placement, current intensity) remain unclear [3].
- **Feasibility of deep brain stimulation (DBS) in routine care**: While **Shi et al. (2026)** reported sustained core symptom reduction in a single case over 12 months, broader applicability, safety in diverse populations, and cost-effectiveness are unproven.
- **Effectiveness of occupational therapy-based water competency interventions**: The **AquOTic trial [8]** aims to assess implementation determinants and costs but has not yet established efficacy for water safety or motor skills in ASD.
- **Association between sleep spindle characteristics and ASD severity**: While **Erçelebi et al. (2026)** identified reduced spindle activity as a potential neurophysiological marker, causal mechanisms and therapeutic targeting remain speculative.

## Evidence limitations

- Findings are drawn from abstracts, registry records and web pages, not full texts; study quality was not appraised from the full papers.
- Doses appear only where an FDA label gives them for this condition (section 2.1); for any other use, see a prescribing source. Approval dates appear only where a retrieved source states them.
- 5 retrieved record(s) were excluded because the relevance reading judged them not to answer the question.
- The web search timed out, so it may be incomplete; the absence of evidence cannot be concluded from it.
