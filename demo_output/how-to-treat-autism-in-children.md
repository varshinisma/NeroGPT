# How to treat autism in children?

**Evidence searched on 2026-10-15** (PubMed, ClinicalTrials.gov, FDA drug labels, web)

## Clinical summary

**Bottom line**
The retrieved evidence supports **structured behavioral and sensory-motor interventions** (e.g., dance, occupational therapy, exercise) to improve motor skills and functional independence in children with autism, alongside **FDA-approved medications** (risperidone and aripiprazole) for irritability. No evidence was found for broad cognitive or social communication improvements beyond targeted protocols.

**Most important recent finding**
Exercise significantly improved overall, gross, and fine motor skills in children with ASD, with dose-response relationships reported [1].

**Immediate context**
Motor skill interventions are evidence-based for functional gains, but no direct evidence supports broad social communication or core ASD symptom treatment. FDA-approved drugs exist for irritability, but no broader ASD symptom management is established in the retrieved data.

## Latest findings

- **Bilateral dual-target deep brain stimulation (DBS) for severe pediatric autism (2026 Dec, Case Reports, indirect evidence)**
 A single 12-month case study of a child with severe autism reported sustained improvements in core symptoms (e.g., ATEC scores reduced by 81.3%, RBS-R by 96.4%) after bilateral DBS targeting the anterior limb of the internal capsule (ALIC) and nucleus accumbens (NAcc). No adverse events were observed, but the findings are limited to one patient and lack generalizability [2].

- **Transcranial direct current stimulation (tDCS) for social function in children and adolescents with autism (2026 Dec, Meta-Analysis/Systematic Review, emerging)**
 A meta-analysis of 13 randomized controlled trials (N=357, ages 4–21 years) found tDCS significantly improved social functioning (standardized mean difference = -0.51) and induced neurofunctional changes, with the largest effects observed via functional near-infrared spectroscopy (fNIRS). Limitations include heterogeneity in protocols and measurement modalities [3].

- **Exercise interventions for motor skills in children with autism (2026-09-18, Meta-Analysis/Systematic Review, emerging)**
The analysis was limited by variability in assessment time points and session durations [1].

- **Modified ketogenic diet for autism (2026-09-11, Randomized Controlled Trial, emerging)**
 A 2-month randomized controlled trial (N=40 young children with autism) found the modified ketogenic diet combined with rehabilitation training significantly reduced ABC and CARS scores compared to rehabilitation alone, with acceptable short-term feasibility. The study’s small sample size and short follow-up limit broader applicability [4].

- **Caregiver-assisted group-based naturalistic developmental behavioral intervention (NDBI) for preschoolers (2026-07-23, Pilot Randomized Controlled Trial, emerging)**
 A pilot trial (N=30 autistic preschoolers) showed meaningful improvements in language, social interaction, and adaptive functioning in both group-based and one-on-one NDBI models, though nonverbal development benefits were only observed in the one-on-one group. The study’s small size and pilot nature restrict preliminary conclusions [5].

## Key studies

1. **Wang et al. (2026)**, meta-analysis: “Exercise significantly improved overall, gross, and fine motor skills in children with ASD.” [1]
2. **Zhao et al. (2026)**, meta-analysis: “Thirteen RCTs were included (N = 357, ages 4-21 years). tDCS significantly improved social functioning (SMD = -0.51, I2 = 0%, p = 0.0002) and induced significant overall neurofunctional modulations (SMD = 1.03, I2 = 71.9%, p = 0.004).” [3]
3. **Sandbank et al. (2023)**, meta-analysis: “When effects were then restricted to exclude those at high risk of detection bias, only one significant summary effect was estimated-naturalistic developmental behavioral interventions on measures of diagnostic characteristics of autism (0.30, 0.03 to 0.57; P=0.03).” [10]
4. **Brignell et al. (2022)**, systematic review: “Most participants were male (range across studies 73% to 87%).” [11]
5. **Fu et al. (2022)**, meta-analysis: “The DD-associated genes were enriched in transcriptomes of progenitor and immature neuronal cells, whereas genes showing stronger evidence in ASD were more enriched in maturing neurons and overlapped with schizophrenia-associated genes, emphasizing that these neuropsychiatric disorders may share common pathways to risk.” [13]

## Conflicting evidence
The **Anderson et al. (2023)** study reports that autistic adults recall traumatic experiences from applied behavior analysis (ABA) interventions, describe long-term negative consequences, and recommend against its use, contradicting the **Hume et al. (2021)** and **Sandbank et al. (2023)** findings that ABA is an established evidence-based practice for improving social communication, adaptive behavior, and reducing autistic symptoms in children. No other direct contradictions were retrieved.

## What remains under investigation
- **Efficacy of dual-target deep brain stimulation (DBS)** for severe pediatric autism: A single 12-month case study [2] reported sustained improvements in core symptoms, but no large-scale trials have reported feasibility or safety across broader populations (advance publication).
- **Optimal dosage of music therapy** for social communication and emotion regulation: A systematic review protocol [8] is under development to synthesize existing data, but no preliminary dose-response relationships have been established.
- **Feasibility of community-based dance programs** for motor skills in autism: A randomized controlled trial [9] is registered but has not yet reported results on engagement or long-term motor skill improvements.
- **Association between blood-based biomarkers (e.g., oxidative stress, inflammatory cytokines) and autism severity**: While Xu et al. (2026) identified knowledge clusters, no causal or predictive biomarkers have been supported for clinical use.
- **Efficacy of sensory integration therapy** for irritability/agitation in autism: Randell et al. (2022) found no significant main effects at 6 or 12 months, but subgroup analyses (e.g., by sex) remain unexplored for potential moderators.

## Evidence limitations

- Findings are drawn from abstracts, registry records and web pages, not full texts; study quality was not appraised from the full papers.
- Doses appear only where an FDA label gives them for this condition (section 2.1); for any other use, see a prescribing source. Approval dates appear only where a retrieved source states them.
- Excluded retracted publication: Efficacy of oral folinic acid supplementation in children with autism spectrum disorder: a (PMID 39243316, 2024). Retracted work is not used as evidence.
- 5 retrieved record(s) were excluded because the relevance reading judged them not to answer the question.
- The web search timed out, so it may be incomplete; the absence of evidence cannot be concluded from it.
