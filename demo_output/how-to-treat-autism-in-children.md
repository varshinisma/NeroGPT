# How to treat autism in children?

**Evidence searched on 2026-10-09** (PubMed, ClinicalTrials.gov, FDA drug labels, web)

## Clinical summary

**Bottom line**
The retrieved evidence supports **intervention-based approaches** for specific domains in autism in children, including **motor skills (exercise, dance, occupational therapy)** and **feeding difficulties (structured, individualized treatment algorithms)**.

**Most important recent finding**
Exercise interventions significantly improved overall, gross, and fine motor skills in children with ASD, with dose-response relationships identified for overall and gross motor skills [1].

**Immediate context**
Evidence is **narrow and intervention-specific**: motor skills and feeding difficulties are supported by structured programs, but no direct evidence exists for broader autism symptom management (e.g., social communication, behavior). FDA-approved drugs (e.g., aripiprazole, risperidone) exist for irritability but are not referenced in the retrieved studies.

## Latest findings
- **2026-09-22**: Case study, a modified ketogenic diet combined with rehabilitation training showed short-term reductions in ABC and CARS scores in young children with ASD compared to rehabilitation alone [2].
- **2026-09-18**: Meta-analysis, exercise significantly improved overall, gross, and fine motor skills in children with ASD, with dose-response relationships identified for overall and gross motor skills [1].
- **2026-09-11**: Randomized controlled trial, a modified ketogenic diet combined with rehabilitation training demonstrated short-term feasibility and greater reductions in ASD symptom scores than rehabilitation alone [2].
- **2026-09-08**: Clinical trial protocol, a community-based dance program for children with autism is under investigation (ANZCTR12625000600448).

## Key studies

1. **Wang et al. (2026)**, meta-analysis: “Exercise significantly improved overall, gross, and fine motor skills in children with ASD.” [1]
2. **Hao et al. (2026)**, systematic review: “There was a significant increase in XR autism research after 2020, with virtual reality (VR) as the largest modality (n = 61, 53.0%), followed by augmented reality (AR) (n = 36, 31.3%).” [6]
3. **Restoy et al. (2024)**, meta-analysis: “Studies of non-pharmacological interventions showed significant improvement in both ER and ED.” [8]
4. **Sandbank et al. (2023)**, meta-analysis: “When effects were then restricted to exclude those at high risk of detection bias, only one significant summary effect was estimated-naturalistic developmental behavioral interventions on measures of diagnostic characteristics of autism (0.30, 0.03 to 0.57; P=0.03).” [9]
5. **Siafis et al. (2022)**, meta-analysis: “There were some indications of improvement by carnosine, haloperidol, folinic acid, guanfacine, omega-3-fatty-acids, probiotics, sulforaphane, tideglusib and valproate, yet imprecise and not robust.” [10]

## Conflicting evidence
None was retrieved.

## What remains under investigation
- **Efficacy**: Head-to-head trials comparing exercise, diet, and behavioral interventions for specific ASD symptoms (e.g., social communication vs. motor skills).
- **Effectiveness**: Long-term outcomes of caregiver-assisted NDBIs and ketogenic diet interventions in diverse clinical settings.
- **Feasibility**: Scalability of extended reality (XR) interventions for routine clinical use in autism treatment.
- **Association**: Exploration of blood-based biomarkers (e.g., oxidative stress, inflammatory cytokines) as predictors of treatment response in ASD.
- **Exploratory**: Dual-target deep brain stimulation’s broader applicability and safety in larger pediatric ASD cohorts.

## Evidence limitations

- Findings are drawn from abstracts, registry records and web pages, not full texts; study quality was not appraised from the full papers.
- Doses appear only where an FDA label gives them for this condition (section 2.1); for any other use, see a prescribing source. Approval dates appear only where a retrieved source states them.
- Excluded retracted publication: Efficacy of oral folinic acid supplementation in children with autism spectrum disorder: a (PMID 39243316, 2024). Retracted work is not used as evidence.
- 4 retrieved record(s) were excluded because the relevance reading judged them not to answer the question.
