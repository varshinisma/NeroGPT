# how to treat cancer in children??

**Evidence searched on 2026-10-09** (PubMed, ClinicalTrials.gov, FDA drug labels, web)

## Clinical summary

**Bottom line**
**Psychosocial and physical interventions** (e.g., cognitive training, exercise) are also supported for quality-of-life outcomes, though their efficacy varies by context.

**Most important recent finding**
Opioids (morphine, fentanyl) are the most commonly used for pediatric cancer pain, with intravenous and enteral routes preferred. [1]

**Immediate context**
**Supportive care** (e.g., pain management, exercise, psychosocial support) is critical but does not replace standard oncology treatments. **No evidence** was retrieved on novel systemic therapies for broad pediatric cancer indications.

## Latest findings
- **2027 Jan 15**: *Emerging evidence* – A fused laser-induced breakdown spectroscopy and Raman spectroscopy method achieved **92.29% accuracy** in identifying childhood brain tumors from dried serum, outperforming standalone techniques [2].
- **2026 Dec 01**: *Emerging evidence* – Morphine and fentanyl remain the most used opioids for pediatric cancer pain, primarily via intravenous or enteral routes, though family member experiences remain understudied [1].
- **2026 Sep 21**: *Emerging evidence* – Psychosocial interventions showed **moderate effect sizes (SMD 0.51)** on neurocognitive outcomes in children with cancer [3].
- **2026 Sep 17**: *Emerging evidence* – Exercise interventions for children with acute leukemia are supported by **17 best-evidence items** across safety, modality, and timing, though long-term individualized programs are recommended [4].

## Key studies

1. **Paterson et al. (2026)**, meta-analysis: “Implications for cancer survivors These findings suggest the promise of interventions for improving neurocognitive, psychological, and HRQOL outcomes in children and adolescents affected by cancer, while also highlighting substantial evidence gaps, particularly for early targeted approaches in the cancer care continuum.” [3]
2. **Chen et al. (2026)**, systematic review: “Compared with sham controls, meridian-based TCM interventions did not significantly reduce nausea severity (SMD: -0.25; 95% CI: -0.78 to 0.28) or vomiting frequency (SMD: -0.37; 95% CI: -0.90 to 0.16).” [6]
3. **Zhou et al. (2026)**, systematic review: “Seventeen items of best evidence were extracted, covering eight dimensions: intervention principles, safety, exercise modalities, intensity, frequency and timing, stage-specific strategies, intervention settings, and outcome improvement.” [4]
4. **Herrera et al. (2024)**, randomized controlled trial: “Of 994 patients who underwent randomization, 970 were included in the intention-to-treat population for efficacy analyses.” [8]
5. **Puusa et al. (2026)**, narrative review: “Conclusions Opioids are particularly significant in managing pain among children with cancer.” [1]

## What remains under investigation
- **Association**: *NCT06175273* (Phase 2/3, completed) examined **nutrition interventions** (oral supplements, tube feed) for body composition, with results posted but specific outcomes not detailed.
- **Experimental**: *NCT01362803* (Phase 1/2, active) investigates **selumetinib (AZD6244)** for NF1-related inoperable PN, focusing on response rates.
- **Effectiveness**: Long-term **quality-of-life outcomes** for children with CNS tumors remain understudied, particularly regarding socioeconomic and family-functioning determinants.
