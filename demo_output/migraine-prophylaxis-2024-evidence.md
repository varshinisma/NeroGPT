# Migraine prophylaxis 2024 evidence?

**Evidence searched on 2026-10-09** (PubMed, ClinicalTrials.gov, FDA drug labels, web)

## Clinical summary

**Bottom line**
Migraine prophylaxis in 2024 is supported by established oral drugs (e.g., beta-blockers, amitriptyline, topiramate) that reduce monthly migraine days by ~1–1.4 days vs placebo, as well as emerging CGRP-targeted therapies (gepants) and procedural options like ultrasound-guided greater occipital nerve block (GONB), which significantly lowers pain intensity and headache frequency. However, most emerging pharmacological targets remain preclinical, and phenotype-stratified approaches (e.g., for vestibular migraine) require further validation.

**Most important recent finding**
Ultrasound-guided greater occipital nerve block (US-GONB) reduced monthly headache frequency by **9.12 days** (95% CI –11.65 to –6.58) in a meta-analysis of 658 patients [1].

**Immediate context**
Established oral prophylactic agents (e.g., beta-blockers, tricyclics, topiramate) are guideline-supported for episodic migraine, while CGRP antagonists (gepants) and monoclonal antibodies are newer but cost/reimbursement barriers persist. Vestibular migraine management lacks robust phenotype-stratified evidence, and most non-pharmacological interventions remain exploratory.

## Latest findings

- **High-dose omega-3 fatty acids (EPA/DHA)** (2024, Network Meta-Analysis)
  In a network meta-analysis of 11 interventions for migraine prophylaxis, high-dose EPA/DHA supplementation demonstrated the largest reduction in migraine days per month (−2.32, 95% CI −3.32 to −1.32) and migraine severity (SMD −2.23, 95% CI −3.17 to −1.30) compared with placebo. The study highlights its favorable acceptability profile, though confidence intervals for acceptability were wide (odds ratio 1.00, 95% CI 0.06 to 17.41) [2].

- **Melatonin** (2026, Meta-Analysis)
A systematic review of 9 randomized controlled trials (n = 788) found melatonin reduced migraine attack duration by 4.98 hours (MD −4.98, 95% CI −9.30 to −0.67) and headache days by 1.54 days (MD −1.54, 95% CI −2.50 to −0.58) versus placebo.

- **Rimegepant for preventive treatment** (2025, Randomized Controlled Trial)
Adverse events were mild, with no liver toxicity signals [3].

- **Atogepant for refractory episodic migraine** (2024, Randomized Controlled Trial)
  In the ELEVATE trial (n = 313), atogepant reduced monthly migraine days by 2.4 days (95% CI −3.2 to −1.5) versus placebo in patients who failed conventional oral prophylaxis. Constipation was the most common adverse event (10% vs. 3% with placebo) [4].

- **Ultrasound-guided greater occipital nerve block (US-GONB)** (2026, Meta-Analysis, advance publication)
  A systematic review of 12 studies (n = 658) found US-GONB reduced monthly headache frequency by 9.12 days (MD −9.12, 95% CI −11.65 to −6.58) and analgesic use by 10.01 units (MD −10.01, 95% CI −12.91 to −7.11). Proximal injections showed numerically larger reductions than distal approaches, though adverse events (e.g., sensory loss) were transient [1].

## Key studies

1. **Pringsheim et al. (2026)**, Systematic Review: For episodic migraine, high-confidence evidence showed that galcanezumab and erenumab are more effective than placebo in reducing headache frequency. Moderate-confidence evidence supported benefit from atogepant, eptinezumab, fremanezumab, propranolol, topiramate, and valproate [5].
2. **Tassorelli et al. (2024)**, Randomized Controlled Trial: In adults with episodic migraine, atogepant reduced monthly migraine days by 4.2 (vs. 1.9 with placebo) over 12 weeks, with constipation as the most common adverse event [4].
3. **Mistry et al. (2024)**, Systematic Review/Network Meta-Analysis: In adults with chronic migraine, calcitonin gene-related peptide monoclonal antibodies (e.g., eptinezumab, fremanezumab) reduced headache/migraine days by 2.0–2.5 per month, outperforming topiramate and onabotulinumtoxinA [6].
4. **Diener et al. (2026)**, Review: Oral prophylactic drugs (e.g., beta-blockers, flunarizine, amitriptyline, topiramate) reduced migraine days/month by 0.44–1.4 vs. placebo. CGRP monoclonal antibodies (e.g., eptinezumab, fremanezumab) reduced days by 0.7–3.8 [8].
5. **Abouelmagd et al. (2026)**, Meta-Analysis: Melatonin reduced migraine attack duration by 4.98 hours and headache days by 1.54 vs. placebo in randomized trials (n=788), with a favorable safety profile [7].

## Conflicting evidence
No direct disagreement was retrieved between studies on migraine prophylaxis efficacy for **adults**. However, **topiramate’s role in women of childbearing age** is contested: while it remains supported by moderate-confidence evidence for chronic migraine [5], recent pharmacovigilance data [9] contraindicate its use during pregnancy and caution its use in non-pregnant women due to neurodevelopmental risks. No other direct contradictions in efficacy or safety were identified.

## What remains under investigation
- **Efficacy of high-dose EPA/DHA** in pediatric migraine prophylaxis: A 2024 network meta-analysis (Tseng et al.) reported compelling efficacy in adults, but **no direct pediatric evidence** was retrieved.
- **Long-term safety and cost-effectiveness** of gepants (e.g., atogepant, rimegepant) in real-world settings: While short-term efficacy is supported by limited evidence [11, 3], **long-term data (beyond 12 weeks)** and comparative cost-effectiveness remain understudied.
- **Head-to-head trials** comparing melatonin vs. standard prophylactic agents (e.g., amitriptyline) for migraine frequency reduction: Abouelmagd et al. (2026) highlighted melatonin’s benefits but noted **no direct comparisons** with first-line agents.
- **Ultrasound-guided greater occipital nerve block (US-GONB) mechanisms**: Sabet et al. (2026) demonstrated efficacy in reducing headache frequency, but **no mechanistic trials** explain why proximal vs. distal injections differ in effect.
- **Feasibility of short-term prevention (STP) strategies** (e.g., rimegepant for perimenstrual migraine): Monteith et al. (2026) described provider perceptions but **no randomized trials** validate STP efficacy vs. traditional prophylaxis.
- **Efficacy of orexinergic pathways or nitric oxide synthase inhibitors** in migraine: Atraszkiewicz et al. (2026) noted mechanistic plausibility but **no clinical trials** demonstrating efficacy were retrieved.
