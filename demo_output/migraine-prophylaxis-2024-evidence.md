# Migraine prophylaxis 2024 evidence?

**Evidence searched on 2026-10-14** (PubMed, ClinicalTrials.gov, FDA drug labels, web)

## Clinical summary

**Bottom line**
The 2024 evidence supports **oral prophylactic drugs (β-blockers, amitriptyline, topiramate, flunarizine) and monoclonal antibodies targeting CGRP** for episodic migraine prophylaxis, with reductions in monthly migraine days (e.g., 1.2–1.4 days vs. placebo). **Ultrasound-guided greater occipital nerve block (US-GONB)** also shows short-term efficacy in reducing pain intensity and headache frequency, though its role remains adjunctive. **Phenotype-guided approaches (e.g., venlafaxine for vestibular migraine)** are under investigation but not yet standardized.

**Most important recent finding**
Ultrasound-guided greater occipital nerve block (US-GONB) significantly reduced monthly headache frequency by **9.12 days** (MD = −9.12, 95% CI [−11.65, −6.58]) and pain intensity (MD = −3.48 points) in a systematic review/meta-analysis of 12 studies (658 patients). [1]

**Immediate context**
Established oral prophylactic agents (β-blockers, tricyclics, anticonvulsants) and CGRP monoclonal antibodies remain the cornerstone for episodic migraine prophylaxis, with clear efficacy in reducing migraine days. **US-GONB is emerging as a non-pharmacological option** but lacks long-term data or FDA approval

## Latest findings

**Ultrasound-guided greater occipital nerve block (US-GONB)** (2026, advance publication, *experimental evidence*)
A systematic review and meta-analysis of 12 studies (658 patients) found that US-GONB significantly reduced pain intensity, monthly headache frequency, headache duration, and analgesic use. The proximal injection approach showed numerically larger reductions in headache frequency than the distal approach, though adverse events (e.g., loss of pinprick sensation, dizziness) were mild and transient [1].

**Melatonin** (2026, *emerging evidence*)
While less potent than amitriptyline, melatonin was well-tolerated and may serve as a supplementary option during preventive agent titration [5].

**Rimegepant for short-term prevention** (2026, *emerging evidence*)
An observational study of 425 U.S. adults found that women were more likely to use rimegepant for short-term prevention due to doctor recommendation, frequency, and severity of attacks. Both sexes reported high satisfaction (≥73%) with the treatment, though men showed numerically higher satisfaction with one-time use [6].

**Dietary supplements (magnesium, CoQ10, riboflavin, etc.)** (2025, *emerging evidence*)
A systematic review and meta-analysis of 22 trials showed that magnesium, CoQ10, riboflavin, and vitamin D reduced migraine frequency, severity, and monthly migraine days compared to placebo. Probiotics also decreased attack frequency and severity, though effects varied by supplement [8].

**Rimegepant for preventive migraine treatment** (2025, *established evidence*)
Adverse events were mild, and no liver toxicity signals were observed [9].

## Key studies

1. **Pringsheim et al. (2026)**, Systematic Review: For episodic migraine, high-confidence evidence showed that galcanezumab and erenumab are more effective than placebo in reducing headache frequency. Moderate-confidence evidence supported benefit from atogepant, eptinezumab, fremanezumab, propranolol, topiramate, and valproate. For chronic migraine, high-confidence evidence supported reductions in headache frequency with fremanezumab, galcanezumab, and onabotulinumtoxinA [11].
2. **Diener et al. (2026)**, Review-Article: Oral prophylactic drugs lowered the number of migraine days per month by 1.27 (beta-blockers), 0.44 (flunarizine), 1.2 (amitriptyline), and 1.4 (topiramate) compared to placebo. Monoclonal antibodies against CGRP or the CGRP receptor reduced migraine days by 0.7–3.8, with atogepant showing 0.7–2.4 fewer migraine days per month [4].
3. **Talandashti et al. (2025)**, Meta-Analysis: Magnesium supplementation reduced migraine attacks by 2.51, severity by 0.88, and monthly migraine days by 1.66 compared to controls [8].

## Conflicting evidence
No direct disagreement was retrieved between studies on migraine prophylaxis efficacy for established treatments (e.g., CGRP monoclonal antibodies or topiramate). However, **Tepper et al. (2025)** notes that melatonin has "very low certainty for evidence of efficacy," while **Abouelmagd et al. (2026)** reports statistically significant reductions in migraine burden with melatonin [12, 5]. The reason for this discrepancy is not established in the retrieved data.

## What remains under investigation
- **Head-to-head comparisons**: Whether CGRP monoclonal antibodies (e.g., eptinezumab vs. fremanezumab) or gepants (e.g., atogepant vs. rimegepant) are superior in specific migraine subtypes (e.g., chronic vs. episodic) remains unanswered.
- **Long-term safety and adherence**: The retrieved data highlights gaps in long-term real-world safety and adherence for gepants and CGRP inhibitors, particularly in lower-resource settings.
- **Dietary supplements vs. pharmacotherapy**: While magnesium, riboflavin, and CoQ10 show promise in emerging evidence, their comparative efficacy and safety against established drugs (e.g., topiramate) are not yet established.
- **Sex-specific responses**: Observational evidence suggests sex differences in satisfaction with rimegepant [6], but efficacy differences by sex are not directly investigated in randomized trials.
- **Cost-effectiveness in diverse populations**: Economic evaluations [7] focus on high-income countries; cost-effectiveness in lower-resource settings or specific subgroups (e.g., older adults) is understudied.
- **Ultrasound-guided nerve blocks**: While proximal vs. distal greater occipital nerve blocks show efficacy [1], their role as a first-line or adjunctive therapy compared to pharmacotherapy is not yet defined.

## Evidence limitations

- Findings are drawn from abstracts, registry records and web pages, not full texts; study quality was not appraised from the full papers.
- Doses appear only where an FDA label gives them for this condition (section 2.1); for any other use, see a prescribing source. Approval dates appear only where a retrieved source states them.
- 3 retrieved record(s) were excluded because the relevance reading judged them not to answer the question.
- The web search timed out, so it may be incomplete; the absence of evidence cannot be concluded from it.
