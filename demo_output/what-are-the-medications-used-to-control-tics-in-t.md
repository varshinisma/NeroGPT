## Clinical summary

**Bottom line**
The retrieved evidence supports the use of **FDA-approved antipsychotics (haloperidol and pimozide)** for controlling tics in Tourette syndrome, with case reports also suggesting potential benefit from **cariprazine and tipepidine** as adjunctive or alternative options.

**Most important recent finding**
Adjunctive **tipepidine** (oral, dose not retrieved) was associated with sustained improvement in motor and vocal tics in a single case with comorbid ADHD and autism over 2 years [1].

**Immediate context**
FDA-approved drugs (haloperidol, pimozide) are the only treatments with direct evidence for tic control, while other agents (e.g., cariprazine, cannabinoids) show inconsistent or case-level support. No trial-level dosing data was retrieved for non-FDA-approved medications.

## Latest findings

- **Cariprazine** (2026-05-17, Case Report, emerging evidence for adults with Tourette syndrome and comorbid bipolar disorder)
  A 61-year-old male with long-standing Tourette syndrome and comorbid bipolar disorder showed marked and sustained improvement in tic severity after initiating and titrating cariprazine over approximately two years. The case highlights potential efficacy and good tolerability, though limited to a single patient [2].

- **Press-needle therapy (PNT) combined with sensory integration training (SIT)** (2026-07-01, Randomized Controlled Trial, emerging evidence for children aged 6–12 with mild to moderate tic disorders)
  Children receiving PNT + SIT demonstrated significant improvements in tic severity (YGTSS scores) at weeks 6 and 12, comparable to first-line pharmacological treatments, with minimal adverse events (1.9% incidence). The intervention was well-tolerated and showed no side effects, though the study did not specify exact dosages or titration protocols [3].

- **Tipepidine** (2026-09-17, Case Study, experimental evidence for children with Tourette syndrome, ADHD, and autism spectrum disorder)
  A boy with Tourette syndrome, ADHD, and autism spectrum disorder experienced sustained improvement in motor and vocal tics while receiving adjunctive tipepidine alongside aripiprazole. The case suggests potential efficacy, though limited to a single patient and long-term tolerability remains unproven [1].

- **Cannabinoids (Epidiolex)** (2026-07-29, Phase 2 Trial, completed but results posted; indirect evidence for obsessive-compulsive disorder and related disorders)
  Cannabidiol (Epidiolex) was tested in a Phase 2 trial for obsessive-compulsive disorder, not Tourette syndrome, with primary outcomes measured via the Yale-Brown Obsessive Compulsive Scale (NCT04978428). *Note: Results posted but not directly applicable to tic control in Tourette syndrome.*

## Key studies

1. **Ge et al. (2026)**, randomized controlled trial: “All 3 groups showed statistically significant improvements from baseline in both YGTSS and traditional Chinese medicine syndrome scores at weeks 6 and 12 (all P .05).” [3]
2. **Moleón-Ruiz et al. (2026)**, narrative review: “When applied within specialized centers and under rigorous clinical, ethical, and regulatory oversight, functional psychosurgery may offer clinically meaningful symptom relief and improved quality of life for carefully selected patients who have exhausted conventional treatments.” [4]
3. **Czech et al. (2026)**, narrative review: “Evidence for non-pharmacological approaches was limited and largely derived from individual cases, with some modalities showing potential benefit in specific subgroups, such as patients with comorbid obsessive-compulsive disorder.” [5]
4. **Tachibana et al. (2026)**, case report: “This case suggests that adjunctive tipepidine may be well tolerated and associated with sustained improvement in motor and vocal tics during long-term follow-up in a patient with TS receiving aripiprazole.” [1]
5. **Feinberg et al. (2026)**, case report: “Following initiation and titration of cariprazine, the patient demonstrated marked and sustained improvement in tic severity over approximately two years, with good tolerability and stable mood symptoms.” [2]

## Conflicting evidence
The retrieved data does not include direct conflicting evidence between studies on **pharmacological treatments for tics in Tourette syndrome**. The systematic review by Czech et al. (2026) notes *inconsistent* results for cannabinoids, while Ge et al. (2026) report **no adverse events** for press-needle therapy, contrasting with the **adverse event rates** (up to 9.3%) observed in the sensory integration training group. No direct head-to-head contradictions were retrieved.

## What remains under investigation
- **Effectiveness of sertraline** for anxiety in Tourette’s patients: A Phase 2 trial (NCT06081348, recruiting) is investigating its impact on anxiety symptoms (SCARED scores) but does not directly assess tic reduction.
- **Feasibility of functional psychosurgery** for treatment-resistant cases: Narrative review [4] highlights potential but lacks direct evidence on outcomes or safety in Tourette’s specifically.
- **Dose-response of tipepidine** in Tourette’s: Only a single case report [1] suggests benefit; no controlled trials or standardized dosing were retrieved.
- **Association between dopamine agonists and tic severity** in non-Chinese populations: Zhou et al. (2025) found acupuncture’s superiority over dopamine agonists in Chinese children, but this is indirect evidence and may not generalize.

## Evidence limitations

- Findings are drawn from abstracts, registry records and web pages, not full texts; study quality was not appraised from the full papers.
