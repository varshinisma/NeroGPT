# What medication is used for insomnia in children?

**Evidence searched on 2026-10-17** (PubMed, ClinicalTrials.gov, FDA drug labels, web)

## Clinical summary

**Bottom line**
The most supported medication for insomnia in children is **melatonin**, particularly as an oral suspension for infants older than 6 months, which significantly reduces nocturnal awakenings and improves sleep continuity after 14 days. Cognitive behavioral therapy for insomnia (CBT-I) is also effective for pediatric sleep disturbances, though its focus varies by condition (e.g., post-concussion symptoms).

**Most important recent finding**

**Immediate context**
Melatonin is the only FDA-labeled drug for pediatric insomnia in the retrieved data, though its use is supported by direct trials in infants. CBT-I is evidence-based for sleep disorders but not specifically for general insomnia in children. No other pharmacologic treatments for pediatric insomnia were directly evaluated in the retrieved evidence.

## Latest findings

- **Melatonin (0.1 mg/kg oral suspension)** in **infants older than 6 months with idiopathic nocturnal irritability** (2026, randomized, single-blind, quasi-experimental trial): A 14-day intervention showed significant reductions in nocturnal awakenings, sleep onset latency, and total nocturnal wakefulness in the melatonin group (n=30) compared to placebo (n=30). No significant changes were observed in the placebo group, though the study was limited by a small sample size and short follow-up [1].

- **Cognitive Behavioral Therapy for Insomnia (CBT-I)** in **children with traumatic brain injury (TBI)** (2026, meta-analysis of 19 studies): Demonstrated efficacy in reducing persistent post-concussion symptoms and improving subjective sleep outcomes, though heterogeneity varied across outcomes. Limitations included reliance on subjective measures and mixed effects across dosage groups [2].

- **Melatonin** in **children with autism or ADHD** (2026, review): Suggested benefits for sleep, but emphasized the need for further research to evaluate efficacy and safety in broader pediatric populations [3].

- **Behavioral sleep interventions** in **autistic children** (2026, systematic review): Provided consistent evidence supporting effectiveness, though methodological weaknesses in most studies limited confidence in findings [4].

- **Melatonin** in **infants with idiopathic nocturnal restlessness** (2026, randomized, single-blind trial): reported efficacy and safety as an adjunctive therapy, with a dose of 0.1 mg/kg showing significant improvements in sleep parameters [1].

## Key studies

1. **Mazurek et al. (2026)**, systematic review: “Existing quantitative research provides consistent evidence supporting the overall effectiveness of behavioral sleep interventions for autistic children.” [4]
2. **Lofitou et al. (2026)**, meta-analysis: “CBT-I demonstrated efficacy in reducing persistent post-concussion symptoms and improving subjective sleep outcomes, although heterogeneity varied across outcomes.” [2]
3. **Kteish et al. (2026)**, narrative review: “While melatonin has shown benefits in children with autism or ADHD, additional research is needed to evaluate the efficacy of other pharmacologic options.” [3]
4. **Gemke et al. (2024)**, narrative review: “Finally, potential long-term consequences and directions for future research are discussed that may improve sleep-related health and well-being.” [5]
5. **Fan et al. (2026)**, systematic review: “Quantitative synthesis showed a significant improvement in sleep quality with syndrome-informed TCM (SMD -2.21, 95% CI -3.26 to -1.16).” [6] *(indirect evidence: different or related population)*

## Conflicting evidence
The **Lofitou et al. (2026)** meta-analysis found that **CBT-I was effective for sleep problems in pediatric TBI**, while **melatonin showed no significant pooled effects** on sleep outcomes. This contrasts with **Safdarian et al. (2026)**, which reported **significant improvements in sleep parameters with melatonin in infants**, suggesting melatonin’s efficacy may vary by population (e.g., TBI vs. idiopathic nocturnal restlessness). No direct head-to-head comparison was retrieved.

## What remains under investigation
- **Efficacy of melatonin in children with insomnia beyond autism/ADHD or TBI populations** (e.g., general pediatric insomnia) – no direct evidence retrieved.
- **Long-term safety and efficacy of pharmacologic treatments** (e.g., melatonin) in children, as most studies have short follow-up (e.g., 14 days).
- **Comparison of CBT-I vs. pharmacologic treatments** (e.g., melatonin) for insomnia in children, as no head-to-head trials were retrieved.
- **Optimal dosing and timing of melatonin** for pediatric insomnia, beyond the **0.1 mg/kg** dose tested in Safdarian et al. (2026).
- **Effectiveness of pharmacologic treatments in children with comorbid conditions** (e.g., anxiety, epilepsy) – indirect evidence suggests variability.
- **Feasibility and acceptability of pharmacologic treatments in routine pediatric care**, as most studies were conducted in controlled settings.

## Evidence limitations

- Findings are drawn from abstracts, registry records and web pages; for 5 open-access papers the methods, results and conclusion were also read, and study quality was not appraised from the full papers.
- Doses appear only where an FDA label gives them for this condition (section 2.1); for any other use, see a prescribing source. Approval dates appear only where a retrieved source states them.
- 2 retrieved record(s) were excluded because the relevance reading judged them not to answer the question.
- The web search timed out, so it may be incomplete; the absence of evidence cannot be concluded from it.
- Not completed within the time limit: the source-aware searches did not finish before the deadline and were not used (the evidence was written from the searches that had finished)
